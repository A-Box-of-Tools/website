1 Pick what to make

A password is a run of characters — short, strong, and something you will keep in a password manager. A passphrase is words picked at random from a list — longer to type, far easier to remember and to read aloud, and the right answer for the handful of secrets you have to type from memory: the password manager's own, your laptop's, your phone's backup code.

The long list is six dice throws a word, so 12.9 bits each. The short list is four throws, 10.3 bits each, and every word in it is five letters or fewer with no word the beginning of another — which is what to use for a passphrase you will be typing on a television remote or reading down a phone screen.

A random digit is the only one of these that adds anything, because it is a fresh choice at every join rather than a rule. The others are about what the box you are pasting into will accept — a space is the most readable and the most likely to be mangled.

What that estimate assumes

That the attacker has stolen the file your password is stored in and is guessing against it on their own hardware at a hundred billion guesses a second — roughly a rack of graphics cards against a fast hash, of the kind that keeps turning up in breaches. Against a password hash chosen properly, bcrypt or Argon2, the same hardware manages a few thousand a second and every answer above becomes about twenty-five bits harder. Against a login form that answers ten times a second, all of them are unreachable.

The figure is deliberately the pessimistic one, and it is the number of guesses to be certain; the average attack finds the answer in half of them, which is what the estimate above uses.
