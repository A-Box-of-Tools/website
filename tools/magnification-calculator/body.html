What will that magnification really show?

Type the numbers printed on a telescope, a pair of binoculars, a microscope or a magnifying glass. The page draws the view they give, to scale, beside what your eye sees alone, and says where a bigger number stops showing anything new.

The numbers on the telescope
mm

The width of the main lens or mirror: the D in “D = 70 mm”, or the first number in a name such as 70/700. An inch is 25.4 mm, so an 8-inch telescope is 203.

mm

The F in “F = 700 mm”. If only an f-number is given, multiply it by the aperture: 130 mm at f/5 is 650 mm.

mm

Engraved on the eyepiece: 25 mm, 10 mm, or a letter and a number such as H20 or SR4. The dial below changes this for you.

A Barlow sits between the telescope and the eyepiece and multiplies the magnification. It adds no detail of its own.

The numbers on the binoculars
×

In 8×42 this is the 8. A zoom model prints a range, as in 10-30×50: try each end of it.

mm

In 8×42 this is the 42. It decides how much light comes in, and so how the view holds up at dusk.

Printed as an angle, such as 7.5°, or as a width at a distance, such as 131 m at 1000 m or 393 ft at 1000 yd. Leave it empty for a typical one.

The numbers on the microscope
Objective
×

The number after the slash on the objective, as in 40×/0.65. It sets the finest detail, and the magnification does not.

×

Engraved on the eyepiece, usually 10×. The dial below changes this for you.

mm

The second number on an eyepiece marked 10×/18. Divided by the objective's magnification it is the width of specimen you see.

The number on the magnifier

A loupe says 10×, a reading glass 2× or 3×, a lamp magnifier often gives diopters, such as +3 or +5, and a bare lens its focal length.

mm

A stronger lens is a smaller one, so it shows less at a time. Leave this empty for a typical width.

    What each number means

    Magnification
    How many times wider everything looks than to your eye alone: the telescope's focal length divided by the eyepiece's.
    Field of view
    How wide a patch of sky fits in the eyepiece. It shrinks as the magnification grows.
    Exit pupil
    The width of the beam of light leaving the eyepiece: aperture divided by magnification. Wider is brighter, up to the width of your own pupil.
    Finest detail
    The smallest thing this aperture can separate, at any magnification. Only a wider lens or mirror makes it smaller.
    Light gathered
    Against your eye's own pupil in the dark. This, and not magnification, is what shows faint things.
    Useful magnifications
    From the lowest that wastes no light to the highest that still helps: about twice the aperture in millimeters, and seldom more than 300× through the air.
    Focal ratio
    Focal length divided by aperture. It says how wide and bright a low-power view can be, and nothing about how much the telescope magnifies.
    Magnification
    The first number. Things look this many times wider, as if you stood that many times closer.
    Front lens
    The second number: the width of each front lens. A wider lens gathers more light and makes the binoculars heavier.
    Exit pupil
    The second number divided by the first: the width of the beam reaching each eye. Your pupil is 2 to 3 mm by day, about 5 mm at dusk and up to 7 mm in the dark.
    Brightness in this light
    Of what your eye alone would get from the same scene. It falls once the beam is narrower than your pupil.
    Field of view
    How much scenery fits across the view. It is printed as an angle or as a width at a set distance.
    Twilight factor
    The square root of the two numbers multiplied together. Makers print it as a guide to detail in poor light. It rewards magnification, while how bright the view stays at dusk depends on the exit pupil.
    In the hand
    Hand shake is magnified with everything else. Up to about 8× it is easy to hold steady, and past about 12× a tripod does more than the extra power.
    Total magnification
    The objective's magnification times the eyepiece's, counted against the same thing held 25 cm from your eye.
    Field of view
    The width of specimen you see: the eyepiece's field number divided by the objective's magnification.
    Finest detail
    The smallest thing this objective can separate, set by its numerical aperture. A stronger eyepiece does not change it.
    Useful magnifications
    From where a keen eye first makes out everything the objective resolves to where there is nothing left to enlarge: about 220 to 1000 times the numerical aperture. Makers quote the upper half of it, 500 to 1000.
    Exit pupil
    The width of the beam leaving the eyepiece. Under about half a millimeter the picture is only being enlarged.
    Magnification
    How many times wider a thing looks than it would held 25 cm from your eye.
    Focal length
    About how far the lens has to be from the object. A stronger lens works closer.
    Diopters
    1000 divided by the focal length in millimeters. Opticians and lamp magnifiers use this number.
    Width you see at once
    About the width of the lens, with your eye close to it. This is why strong magnifiers show so little at a time.
    Finest detail
    What a keen eye can separate through it: the eye's own limit divided by the magnification.

      How did we work this out?

      The numbers you type are taken as printed. Everything else is arithmetic from the figures below, which you can change or check.

      The widest your pupil opens, used for the night sky. It narrows with age. By day it is taken as 2.5 mm and at dusk as 5 mm.

      A telescope eyepiece's apparent field. A wider one shows more sky at the same magnification, and nothing in the view gets bigger.

      The barrel limits how wide a long eyepiece's field can be: about 27 mm across in a 1.25 inch barrel and 46 mm in a 2 inch one.

      Two sums are in use. Dividing 250 mm by the focal length has the eye relaxed, and is how a loupe marked with a power is usually counted. Adding one has the eye focused as near as it can, and is how lamp and reading magnifiers marked in diopters are sold: +3 diopters as 1.75×. Unless you choose one of them here, the page takes whichever goes with the kind of number you typed.

      One blur, three rules of thumb. Any lens spreads a point of light into a small disc, and the eyepiece magnifies that disc with everything else. By Rayleigh's rule for green light, the disc looks about 2.3 arcminutes wide through an exit pupil of 1 mm, and wider as the exit pupil shrinks. A keen eye separates 1 arcminute. So with an exit pupil above 2.3 mm your eye, not the lens, limits what you see; at 1 mm everything the lens resolves is comfortably large; and under 0.5 mm the blur itself is all that grows. For a telescope those are magnifications of the aperture in millimeters divided by 2.3, the aperture itself, and twice the aperture. For a microscope they are about 217, 500 and 1000 times the numerical aperture.

      Telescopes. Magnification is the telescope's focal length divided by the eyepiece's, times any Barlow. The exit pupil is the aperture divided by the magnification. The finest detail is 138 arcseconds divided by the aperture in millimeters, and Dawes' older figure for splitting double stars, 116 divided by the aperture, is about a sixth tighter. The field of view is the eyepiece's apparent field divided by the magnification, capped by its barrel. Above about 300× the unsteadiness of the air usually hides whatever the telescope could add.

      Binoculars. The exit pupil is the front lens width divided by the magnification, and the brightness is the square of the exit pupil over your own pupil, never more than 1. A field given as a width is turned into an angle exactly, so 131 m at 1000 m and 393 ft at 1000 yd are both 7.5°. The circle drawn is the magnification times that angle; a newer standard that works in tangents gives about five degrees less for the same pair.

      Microscopes. Total magnification is objective times eyepiece. The finest detail is 0.61 times the wavelength divided by the numerical aperture, 0.34 µm divided by the numerical aperture in green light. The width of specimen in view is the field number divided by the objective's magnification, and an eyepiece with no number engraved is taken as 180 mm divided by its magnification, which is 18 mm at 10×, and never more than the 20 mm a standard eyepiece tube allows. A lamp can always be turned up, so these pictures are not dimmed.

      Magnifiers. A simple lens is counted against the same object held 250 mm from the eye: 250 mm divided by its focal length with the eye relaxed, and one more than that with the eye focused close. Its focal length is 1000 mm divided by its diopters. The patch seen through it is taken as the lens's own width, which holds with the eye about one focal length behind it.

      The pictures. All three are drawn to one scale of angle: the circle is the eyepiece's field as your eye meets it, and everything in it is as wide as the magnification makes it. The close look uses one screen pixel for two arcminutes of the view, which on a phone or laptop at its usual distance is close to life size. The softness in it is the blur described above. A surface dims as the square of the exit pupil over your pupil, and the picture darkens only once that has used up the light the subject has to spare: the full Moon is taken as 2,500 candelas per square meter and a view as dull below 2, with Jupiter and Saturn fainter in proportion to the sunlight they receive and reflect, a sunlit scene as 5,000 and one at dusk as 3. Stars are points, so they do not dim this way; those too faint for the light gathered are left out.

      What the drawings are. The Moon is 3,475 km wide and 384,400 km away, with its seas and about twenty of its largest craters where they are and smaller ones scattered by rule. Jupiter and Saturn are at their mean distances at opposition, 629 and 1,277 million km, and Jupiter's moons and Titan are at typical places along orbits they travel in days. The Pleiades are the eleven brightest stars from the catalogue. The bird is 14 cm long and 20 m away. On the slide, an onion cell is 0.3 mm long, a red blood cell 7.5 µm, a hair 0.07 mm, a grain of table salt about 0.3 mm and a bacterium 2 µm. The print is 8-point and 5-point type above lines of microprint a quarter of a millimeter high.
