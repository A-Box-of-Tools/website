    How did we work this out?

    The count you enter is exact. Everything else here is an estimate from the figures below, which you can change or check.

    Stacked flat, 15 cm wide. The stack is drawn at its true height and wider than real once it would otherwise vanish.

    English text: roughly 0.75 words per token. This is a writing equivalent, not a claim about books actually read or written.

    A metric dropper gives about 20 drops per milliliter. A dripping tap’s drop is larger, and is often taken as about 0.25 mL.

    Chosen spherical grains, with 60% grains and 40% air. Real sand has mixed shapes and sizes.

    Counting continuously, without sleep or breaks. One year is 365.25 days.

    Books. Heights are rounded: a person 1.7 m, a table 0.75 m, a house 8 m, the Statue of Liberty 93 m from the ground to the torch, the Eiffel Tower 330 m, the Burj Khalifa 828 m, Mount Everest 8,849 m. Distances are measured straight up from the ground: an airliner’s cruising height 11 km, the Space Station about 400 km, the Moon 384,400 km, Mars about 54.6 million km at its closest, the Sun 149.6 million km, Neptune 4.5 billion km, the Voyager 1 spacecraft about 25.9 billion km and still moving, Proxima Centauri 4.24 light-years, the Orion Nebula about 1,500 light-years, the Andromeda galaxy 2.5 million light-years, the Virgo Cluster 54 million, the quasar 3C 273 about 2.5 billion, and the edge of the observable universe about 46.5 billion. The far side of the Milky Way is taken as its width, about 100,000 light-years, because the galaxy is drawn at that size around the foot of the stack. The library landmarks are numbers of books, so they move with thickness: about 25.8 million cataloged books at the Library of Congress in 2023, and Google’s 2010 estimate of about 130 million different books in the world.

    Water and sand. The colored area is the true share of the container’s drawn shape, and the containers themselves are illustrations rather than one physical ruler. Capacities are chosen examples: a 15 mL spoon, a 250 mL cup, a 10 L bucket, a 200 L bath, a 5 × 3 × 2 m room, a 50 × 25 × 2 m pool and a supertanker of about 320,000 m³. The Great Pyramid is taken as about 2.6 million m³, and the example mountain as a cone 1 km tall with a 1 km base radius, which is a chosen shape and not a measurement of a real mountain. Loch Ness holds about 7.4 km³, Lake Superior about 12,100 km³, and all the water on Earth about 1.386 billion km³. The Moon, Earth and Neptune are their whole volumes.

    There is no single exact count for “all the sand on Earth” here. A defensible estimate first needs a scope, such as beaches or deserts, and assumptions about grain size and depth.

    Stars. Star counts are rough by nature, and these are the published round figures rather than measurements of every star: about 9,100 stars bright enough for a dark sky without a telescope, across the whole sky; about 10 million in Omega Centauri; about 100 billion in the Milky Way, whose true figure may be several times that; about 1 trillion in Andromeda; and 10²³ for the observable universe, the middle of a rough range of 10²² to 10²⁴. Once there are too many to draw, each dot stands for a stated number of stars, and the lit share is the share of that shape’s dots, from the middle outward.

    Time. Spans are rounded: a human lifetime of 80 years, about 4,500 years since the Great Pyramid was built, about 300,000 years of Homo sapiens, about 66 million years since the dinosaurs died out, the Earth’s age of about 4.54 billion years and the universe’s of about 13.8 billion.

    Read the references: OpenAI’s English token estimate · USGS grain-size classification · USGS on all the water on Earth · EPA on the Great Lakes · NASA planetary fact sheets · ESA on how many stars there are · Library of Congress collection figures. These links are optional reading; the calculator does not contact them.
