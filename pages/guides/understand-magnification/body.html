A magnification is how much closer it looks

A magnification says how many times wider something looks than it does to your eye alone, which is the same as how many times closer it seems. Binoculars marked 8× make a bird 20 m away look as it would from 2.5 m. A telescope at 70× brings the Moon from 384,400 km to about 5,500 km. For something small enough to hold, the comparison is with holding it 25 cm from your eye, so a red blood cell at 400× looks as big as a 3 mm bead at reading distance.

Open the calculator, choose the instrument and type the numbers printed on it. The pictures are drawings worked out from those numbers, to scale. They are not photographs, and a real view also depends on the air, the quality of the optics and your own eyes.

A bigger number shows a smaller patch

The first picture is the circle you look into. An eyepiece shows a circle of a fixed width, so the more the view is magnified, the less of the world fits in it. A common telescope eyepiece at 70× takes in about three quarters of a degree of sky, which is one and a half Moons. At 140× it takes in half that, and the Moon no longer fits. Binoculars print this as an angle, such as 7.5°, or as a width at a distance, such as 131 m at 1000 m. On a microscope it is the eyepiece's field number divided by the objective's magnification: 18 mm over 40× is 0.45 mm of specimen.

Planets are the surprise. Saturn with its rings is 44 arcseconds wide, so even at 130× it spans about 3% of the view. That is how it really looks, and it is why the second picture exists: a close look at the middle of the view, drawn at about the size your eye would meet it.

The lens decides the detail, and the number only spreads it out

Any lens or mirror blurs a point of light into a tiny disc, and the narrower the lens, the wider the disc. The eyepiece magnifies that blur along with everything else. So there is a magnification at which you can already see all the detail the lens collected, and beyond it the picture only gets bigger, softer and dimmer. That is called empty magnification.

The number to watch is the exit pupil: the width of the beam of light leaving the eyepiece, which is the width of the main lens divided by the magnification. Above about 2.3 mm your eye, and not the instrument, limits what you see, so more power still shows more. At 1 mm everything the lens resolves is comfortably large. Under half a millimeter there is nothing new to show. For a telescope that last point is twice its aperture in millimeters: 120× for a 60 mm telescope and 400× for a 200 mm one.

Check a telescope's box before you buy it

This is the commonest use. Type the aperture and focal length from the listing, then the shortest eyepiece and any Barlow lens it comes with, which is how the headline magnification was reached. Read the verdict under the dial and the row called Useful magnifications. A 60 mm telescope tops out near 120× and a 130 mm one near 260×, and the air itself seldom allows more than about 300× whatever you buy.

Then look at the list of the same telescope with other eyepieces. The ones marked sharpest are the ones you will use most, and if every eyepiece in the kit lands in the red, a longer one will do more for the view than anything else you could add. When two telescopes are close in price, the wider one shows finer detail and fainter things at every magnification.

Read both numbers on binoculars

In 8×42 the 8 is the magnification and the 42 is the width of each front lens in millimeters. Divide the second by the first for the exit pupil, here 5.25 mm. Your own pupil is 2 to 3 mm wide in daylight and opens to about 5 mm at dusk. So by day a pocket 8×21 looks nearly as bright as the larger pair, and at dusk it passes about a quarter of the light your eye could use.

The first number has a cost as well. The shake of your hands is magnified with everything else: up to about 8× is easy to hold steady, 10× to 12× takes braced elbows, and beyond that a tripod shows more than the extra power does. Higher power also narrows the view, which makes a moving bird harder to find.

Read both numbers on a microscope objective

An objective marked 40×/0.65 magnifies 40 times and has a numerical aperture of 0.65. Multiply the first number by the eyepiece's, usually 10×, for the total of 400×. The second number sets the finest detail, about a third of a micrometer divided by it, which is half a micrometer here. The useful range of total magnification is roughly 500 to 1000 times the numerical aperture, so 325× to 650× for this objective.

That is why 1000× can mean two different things. Reached with a 25× eyepiece on the 40×/0.65 objective it is empty, and bacteria are larger blurs. Reached with a 100×/1.25 oil-immersion objective and the ordinary 10× eyepiece it is useful, and the same bacteria are distinct rods. Try both examples and compare the close looks.

A magnifying glass in powers, millimeters and diopters

A single lens is described three ways. Its magnification is 250 mm divided by its focal length, and its strength in diopters is 1000 divided by the focal length in millimeters, so a 10× loupe has a 25 mm focal length and 40 diopters. Some makers count the eye as focused close and add one, which is why the same +20 diopter lens is sold as both 5× and 6×, and why a lamp magnifier of +3 diopters is called 1.75×. The focal length is also roughly how far from the object the lens has to be held, and a stronger lens has to be smaller, so it shows less at a time: a 10× loupe covers about 2 cm.

Use it to plan a night or a lesson

Pick what you want to look at and move the dial until it fits. The bright stars of the Pleiades span a degree and want a low power or binoculars. The Moon fills an ordinary eyepiece at about 100×. Jupiter's four large moons appear in any binoculars, and its two dark belts show from about 50×. A teacher can put the three pictures on a screen, ask a class what doubling the magnification will change, and then drag the dial through the colored zones to find out.

What these numbers do not cover

A camera's zoom figure is the ratio between its longest and shortest focal lengths, so a 10× zoom says nothing about how large things look. A digital or USB microscope's figure depends on the size of the screen it is shown on. Neither can be compared with the magnifications here, and for both the field of view is the better guide. Every limit on this page is also a rule of thumb for good optics in good conditions: a cheap eyepiece, an unsteady night or a tired eye will fall short of it.
