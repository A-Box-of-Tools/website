Give the number something to count

Start by choosing grains of sand, AI text tokens or things to count. The number stays the same, but its meaning decides which comparisons are useful. Try the billion-grain example first, then change the amount by ten.

A billion grains can fit in eleven buckets

With spherical grains 0.5 mm across, and a container holding 60% grains and 40% air, a billion grains occupy about 109 liters. That is roughly eleven 10-liter buckets. The grains are tiny, which is why a huge count can occupy a modest space.

These are chosen assumptions. Sand contains mixed sizes and shapes. The USGS grain-size classification shows how broadly sand sizes vary. Change the grain diameter in the assumptions panel to explore that difference.

Change one thing at a time

Doubling the grain diameter makes each spherical grain eight times larger by volume. Multiplying the count by 1,000 makes a cube holding the sand ten times wider. Volume and length do not grow at the same rate.

The drawings are counting pictures. Their labels say how many reference objects each picture represents. A marked final picture represents the stated fraction of one group; it does not claim that a fraction of a mountain's height equals that fraction of its volume.

Tokens can be writing or an imagined pile

The writing view uses the rough English estimate of 0.75 words per token. One million tokens therefore correspond to about 750,000 words: ten example books if each book contains 75,000 words. Change the example book length to suit the kind of writing you have in mind. Other languages and tokenizers vary; see OpenAI's explanation of tokens.

“As sand” asks a different question: imagine one grain for every token. Tokens have no physical volume of their own. A writing equivalent also does not establish how many books a model read or how much unique writing it produced.

Try time when space feels surprising

At one item each second, a billion items take about 31.7 years to count. The time view assumes continuous counting, without sleep or breaks, and uses 365.25 days per year. Change the rate to compare one, ten or a hundred items each second.

Make the mountain a defined example

The example mountain is a cone 1,000 meters tall with a 1,000-meter base radius. It is a chosen shape, not a measurement of a real mountain. Use the follow-up question to find how many grains would fill it with the current grain size.

There is no exact worldwide sand count in this tool. Such an estimate first needs a scope, such as beaches or deserts, and evidence about area, depth and grain size. A large precise-looking number cannot substitute for those choices.

Explore together and keep the explanation

Ask a child to predict whether the sand would fit in the named container, reveal the answer, then try ten times more. Success is being able to describe the amount using something familiar, rather than remembering all the zeros.
