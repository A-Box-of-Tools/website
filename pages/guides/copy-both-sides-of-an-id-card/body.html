The short answer

Photograph the front and the back of the card on a surface darker than it, from straight above, with no reflection on it. Open ID Card Copy, add the two photos, check the corners it found on each, and save the PDF. Print it with the printer set to 100% or “actual size”, and lay the card on the paper: the two should be the same size.

If the copy is going to a website rather than a printer, choose “No sheet” and a JPEG, and you get one picture of both sides to upload.

Taking the two photos

Four things, in order of how much difference they make:

  1. No reflection. Most cards are laminated or glossy, and a ceiling light or the phone's flash leaves a white patch where the name or the number should be. Nothing can recover what is under a reflection, because it was never photographed. Turn the flash off, and tilt the card or step aside until the patch moves off it.
  2. A background that is not the card's colour. The card is found by its edges, and an edge is where one thing becomes another. A pale card on a white desk has almost no edge to find; on a dark table, a book cover or a pair of jeans it has a clean one all the way round.
  3. Fill the frame. Small print on a card is small: a card that fills a third of the photo can come out too soft to read. Step 2 of the tool says how much detail each photo holds, in dots per inch at the card's real size; under about 150 the print will blur.
  4. Straight above, roughly. It does not have to be exact — undoing the angle is what the tool is for — but a card photographed almost edge-on is mostly foreshortening, and there is not much card left in it to straighten.

Why a copy comes out the wrong size

Almost always because of one setting in the print dialog. “Fit to page”, “shrink to fit” or “scale to fit” makes the whole sheet a few per cent smaller so that it clears the printer's margins, and the card on it shrinks with it. A card that was exactly 85.6 millimetres wide in the file comes out at about 82. Choose “actual size” or 100% instead.

The PDF this tool saves places each side at the card's size in the file's own measurements, so at 100% it prints at that size on any printer. A picture file is less reliable: the copy records its own resolution, and some programs print by it, but many photo viewers fit any picture to the paper whatever it says. For printing, use the PDF.

The other cause is the photo itself. A card photographed at an angle is narrower at the far end, and a tool that crops the rectangle it can see and stretches it into a box gives you a card that is subtly squashed. Here the four corners are mapped onto a rectangle of the card's exact shape, so every line that is straight on the card is straight on the copy.

When the corners are wrong

The corners are a starting point. Press anywhere on the photo and the nearest one comes to your finger; drag it onto the card. Cards have rounded corners, so put each one where the two straight edges would meet rather than on the curve. From the keyboard, Tab reaches a corner and the arrow keys move it.

The usual reasons it goes wrong are a card on a surface of nearly its own colour, and a card with a dark band running close to its edge, whose inner edge can look more like the edge of the card than the card's own does. A darker background fixes the first; for the second, drag the two corners concerned onto the real edge.

If a side comes out upside down or on its end, the turn buttons put it right. Some licences are printed upright rather than across; turn that side once and it is laid out upright on the sheet.

Writing what the copy is for

A copy of your identity card is most of what somebody needs to open an account, rent a flat or sign up for a contract in your name. The copy you hand to a landlord is safest if it can only ever have been for that landlord. Type something like “Copy for the tenancy at 12 Elm Street only, March 2027” into “Words across the card”, and it is written in slanting rows across both sides.

It is drawn into the picture of the card itself, not laid on top of the PDF, so it cannot be deleted from the copy afterwards. Keep it light enough that the card can still be read, because the person you are giving it to still needs to read it. An office that cannot accept a marked copy will say so.

Why this does not need a server

Everything here — finding the card, straightening it, laying it out and writing the PDF — is arithmetic on pixels the browser has already decoded. There is nothing for a server to do, so the tool has none: the page cannot send the photos anywhere, and it works with the network turned off. That matters for this document more than any other.
