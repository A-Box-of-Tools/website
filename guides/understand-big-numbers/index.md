# How to make a big number feel familiar

A billion books stand taller than two Earths, while a billion drops barely wet the floor of a pool. Give the number a meaning, watch it grow beside something familiar, and keep the assumptions where you can see them.

[Open the Number Explorer](https://abox.tools/number-explorer/): Type any number and watch it grow: a stack of books, a pour of water, a sky of stars, a pile of sand, a very long count.

Last updated 3 November 2026

## Start with one number and watch it grow

Type a number or drag the dial, which moves through powers of ten. The picture starts small and the camera pulls back as the number climbs, so you see the size arrive rather than just read it. The same number can then be pictured as books, water, stars, sand or time. Each says what one of them is, because the meaning decides which comparisons are useful.

## A billion books stand taller than two Earths

Stacked flat at 3 cm each, a billion books stand about 30,000 km high. That is more than twice the width of the Earth and about 8% of the way to the Moon. The stack and its landmarks are drawn on one true ruler, from a table to the edge of the observable universe, so the Eiffel Tower and Mount Everest shrink past as the camera pulls back. The only exception is the stack's width, which is drawn wider than real once it would otherwise vanish.

## A billion drops barely wet the floor of a pool

At 0.05 mL each, the size a dropper gives, a billion drops make 50,000 liters. That is about 250 bathtubs, but only 2% of an Olympic pool, about 4 cm deep. Water pours into one named container after another, and when one overflows the next arrives. The colored area is the true share of the container's shape, so a pyramid a tenth full is not filled a tenth of the way up. The containers themselves are illustrations rather than one physical ruler.

## A billion stars is a small part of one galaxy

About 9,100 stars across the whole sky are bright enough to see without a telescope, and up to that number every dot is one star. After that each dot stands for a stated number of stars, in a shape you could really find them in. A billion is about 1% of the Milky Way, taking [ESA's round figure of 100 billion](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Herschel/How_many_stars_are_there_in_the_Universe), and the part that stays dark is the point: it is everything the number has not reached. Star counts are rough by nature, and the true figure for the galaxy may be several times higher.

## A billion grains can fit in eleven buckets

With spherical grains 0.5 mm across, and a container holding 60% grains and 40% air, a billion grains occupy about 109 liters. That is roughly eleven 10-liter buckets. The grains are tiny, which is why a huge count can occupy a modest space.

These are chosen assumptions. Sand contains mixed sizes and shapes. The [USGS grain-size classification](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1991/0375a/report.pdf) shows how broadly sand sizes vary. Change the grain diameter in the assumptions panel to explore that difference.

## Change one thing at a time

Doubling the grain diameter makes each spherical grain eight times larger by volume. Multiplying the count by 1,000 makes a cube holding the sand ten times wider. Volume and length do not grow at the same rate, which is why a thousand times more water looks so different from a thousand times more books.

Press × 10 and the old size stays marked on the picture, labeled as before, so the step you just took is in view. The landmarks beside your number say how many times bigger or smaller each one is, and choosing one jumps to the count that reaches it.

## Tokens can be writing

In books, each one can also be a word or an AI text token. This uses the rough English estimate of 0.75 words per token, so one million tokens correspond to about 750,000 words: ten example books if each book contains 75,000 words, in a stack about 30 cm high. Change the example book length to suit the kind of writing you have in mind. Other languages and tokenizers vary; see [OpenAI's explanation of tokens](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/4936856-understanding-and-counting-tokens).

Tokens have no physical volume of their own, so picturing them as water or stars only imagines one drop or star for each. A writing equivalent also does not establish how many books a model read or how much unique writing it produced.

## Try time when space feels surprising

At one item each second, a billion items take about 31.7 years to count, and a tally keeps running from the moment the picture opens, so you can watch how little of the way the first minute gets you. The timeline assumes continuous counting, without sleep or breaks, and uses 365.25 days per year. Change the rate to compare one, ten or a hundred items each second.

## Make the mountain a defined example

The example mountain is a cone 1,000 meters tall with a 1,000-meter base radius. It is a chosen shape, not a measurement of a real mountain. Choose it among the landmarks to find how many grains or drops would fill it.

There is no exact worldwide sand count in this tool. Such an estimate first needs a scope, such as beaches or deserts, and evidence about area, depth and grain size. A large precise-looking number cannot substitute for those choices.

## Explore together and keep the explanation

Ask a child whether ten times more will reach the next landmark, press yes or no, and watch the answer arrive. Success is being able to describe the amount using something familiar, rather than remembering all the zeros.
